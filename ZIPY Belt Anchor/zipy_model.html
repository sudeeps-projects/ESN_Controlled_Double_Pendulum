Fitting it, in order

Why an open belt still drives it

Trace the strand in Drive the cart. From the post at one end of the cart it runs to the motor pulley, wraps it, comes back through the cart's own corridor, wraps the encoder pulley, and returns to the post at the other end. That is a closed loop that happens to pass through the cart instead of past it.

So turning the motor one way shortens one anchored run and pays out the other by exactly as much. The cart has nowhere to go but along. No dead band, and no slip, because the belt is captured mechanically at the cart rather than gripped by friction.

Both pulleys here are 20T, so the return run is parallel to the rail rather than converging the way it would with a mismatched pair. It sits 6.8 mm off the rail's centre line at every cart position, with 8.5 mm to spare inside the corridor.

Why the encoder belongs at the far end, not on the motor. A 37 mm gearbox has a degree or two of backlash. On the motor shaft that slop would sit inside your position measurement. Out here it is outside the measurement entirely — the encoder reads the belt, and the belt is bolted to the cart, so you read true cart position whatever the gearbox does.

Holding the tension. Belt tension pulls both end brackets inward, so both have to be bolted to the rail. Switch to Clamp the ends above and scrub through it — there is nothing to print and nothing to drill, because the hold-down is already designed into both brackets.

Clamping the two ends

None of the spare printed parts helps here. 09_edge_clip, 08_slot_test_coupon, 03_pulley_end_feet and 07_limit_switch_mount_b have no bolt holes in them at all — the only hole in that set belongs to 11_dc_motor_end_no_feet, and that is its own hold-down, not a clamp. What you need is two bolts and two T-nuts, about £3.

Bolt length matters more than you would think. You are clamping 3 mm of bracket plus the 1.5 mm slot mouth plus the nut. With a 3 mm T-nut use M5 × 8; with a 6 mm drop-in nut, M5 × 10 or 12. Too long is worse than too short: it bottoms out on the back of the slot, feels tight, and clamps nothing at all.

If you have nothing to hand today, two or three zip ties cinched hard around the rail against each bracket's inboard face is a genuine positive stop, and will take far more than the 20 N or so the belt pulls with. A plain M4 or M5 hex nut slid into a side slot from the open end of the rail, with a bolt run into it, does the same job and is reusable.

Worth checking if the swing-up misbehaves. If a bracket creeps inward the belt goes slack, and a slack toothed belt lets the pulley spin underneath it. Swing-up reverses the cart twice a second at full duty, which is by far the hardest this belt has been worked — so if it works and then degrades over a few minutes, look here before you touch the gains.

Bolting the motor and encoder to their brackets

Clamping the brackets to the rail is only half of it. The motor and the encoder each bolt to their bracket's top plate, and both plates carry the same two features, measured off the STLs:

A spigot hole in the middle — Ø12.5 at the motor end, Ø21.0 at the encoder end. The device's raised boss drops into it, and this is what takes the sideways load from the belt. The screws only stop it turning and falling out.

A ring of Ø3.6 mm M3 clearance holes on a Ø31 circle — six of them at the motor end, every 60°, and four at the encoder end at ±60° and ±120°. Use whichever ones line up with the threaded holes in your device's face. If your encoder has the usual three at 120° spacing, two of the three will land; that is plenty, and you should not try to force the third.

Both plates are 4.0 mm thick, so M3 × 8 is the length to buy: 4 mm of plate leaves 4 mm of thread engagement. M3 × 10 only if you have checked your threads go deeper than 6 mm — on a 37 mm gearmotor they often do not, and a screw that bottoms out feels tight while clamping nothing.

The encoder spigot is a loose fit on purpose. Ø21.0 around a Ø20 boss leaves half a millimetre of play all round, so the encoder can sit off-centre. Before you tighten its screws, sight along the belt and push the encoder until its pulley lines up with the motor pulley — otherwise the belt runs at an angle and climbs a flange.

None of this stops a pulley slipping on its shaft. Bolting the encoder down rigidly and then finding the cart moves while the count does not means the pulley's grub screw is not biting. It wants to land on the shaft's flat, and if the shaft is plain round, file one.

Fitting the pole to the encoder

Switch to Fit the pole above and drag the slider to put it together. The order is:

1. Press the M3 nuts into the hub's hex pockets and the slot beside its collar. A drop of superglue stops them falling out while you work.

2. Encoder into the cart's plate — body inboard, toward the rail, boss pushed out through the Ø21 hole, shaft pointing away from the rail. Turn it so the cable exits upward, then three or four M3 × 8 through the plate into its threaded face.

3. Bolt the pole to the hub first, off the machine: 2 × M3 × 16 through link 1's lower holes, heads on the link side. Then link 2 onto link 1's top end, 2 × M3 × 12 through the holes at 208 and 226 mm.

4. Slide the whole hub-and-pole assembly onto the 6 mm shaft, leaving about 1 mm between the collar and the encoder's boss. A business card is the right spacer.

5. This is the step that actually holds it. Run the M3 set screw up through the hub's short end face into that captive nut. It presses on the shaft and clamps the hub to it. Sliding the hub on is not enough — without this screw the pole spins freely on the shaft and the encoder reads nothing. If the shaft is plain round, file a small flat where the screw tip lands first, or it can creep round over time and your angle zero drifts.

The bore runs right through the hub, so you can see the shaft end through it and check how far on you are. It stops 3 mm short of the pole face — the shaft never touches the link, and the hub does all the clamping.

Belt height is the one thing to get right. The cart's slot spans 4–13 mm above the rail's top face, and the encoder bracket's cavity spans 4–20 mm. Slide both pulleys along their shafts until the middle of the belt sits about 9–10 mm above the rail's top face.

The second pendulum, with your parts

ZIPY's own elbow, zipy_dp_elbow, is a joggled bracket. One plate bolts to link 1's top two holes. The other, about 37 mm further out, is an encoder face: a Ø21 hole and six M3 slots on a Ø31 circle, the same pattern as your cart-encoder bracket. ZIPY meant a third optical encoder to go there.

Your two 608 bearings (8 × 22 × 7 mm) need a Ø22 seat 7 mm deep, so they go in a small printed cartridge that bolts in where the encoder would. The shaft runs out through the Ø21 hole to link 2 on a reprinted zipy_dp_hub with an 8 mm bore. The magnet and the AS5048A sit on the inner end, in the space the encoder body would have used. The sensor reads link 2's angle relative to link 1; the firmware adds link 1's angle.

If you have not printed ZIPY's elbow yet, the one-piece block does the same job in one part. Switch between them with the buttons under the model. Use Fit the elbow to take either apart, or click a step below.

Wiring the AS5048A

Run it from 3.3 V. The Teensy's pins are not 5 V tolerant, and the board's outputs swing to whatever it is powered from. Many of these boards default to 5 V, so check yours for a 3.3 V jumper or pad before connecting a signal wire.

PWM: 3 wires (3V3, GND, PWM)Teensy pin 3 · ~1 kHz, one new angle per ms · start here
SPI: 6 wires (3V3, GND, CS, CLK, MISO, MOSI)pins 10, 13, 12, 11 · full 14-bit, read every loop
Already in usepole 1/2 (fixed) · motor 5/6 · cart 8/9

Why PWM first: three wires are the easiest to get through a loose loop or a slip ring, and a 1 kHz update is fast enough at a 500 Hz control rate. Move to SPI if the readings prove noisy or too slow.
